## Short answer

- **The corporate debtor's s.14 moratorium does not cover its personal guarantor.** See *SBI v. V. Ramakrishnan* (SC, 2018), as quoted in *P. Mohanraj* para 13 *(corpus: 2021_INSC_133)*. *P. Mohanraj* para 77 adds that the moratorium protects only the corporate debtor; natural persons stay liable.
- **A resolution plan for the company does not by itself discharge the guarantor** (*Lalit Kumar Jain*, SC, 2021). The guarantee is an independent contract.
- **Section 32A protects only the corporate debtor and property covered by the plan.** Promoters, directors and others involved stay liable to prosecution, and their own property stays open to attachment (*Manish Kumar*, SC, paras 253-254 and 257) *(corpus: 2021_INSC_28)*. *Nitin Jain* (Del HC, 2021) para 101(Q) *(corpus: DLHC010126002021)* says the same for liquidation.
- **So a promoter-guarantor under PMLA investigation gets no shelter** from the company's moratorium, plan or s.32A. ED can attach his personal proceeds of crime and prosecute him whatever happens to the company.
- **In the guarantor's own Part III insolvency, ss.96 and 101 impose a moratorium on "debts".** No case in this set decides whether that moratorium reaches a PMLA attachment. *LawBase analysis:* it should not, for the reason given in *Axis Bank* (Del HC) para 141 *(corpus: DLHC012396152018)* and *P. Mohanraj* para 76: ED is not a creditor and proceeds of crime are not a debt. Expect the point to be argued.
- **Substantive consolidation of group companies rests on the NCLT's equitable jurisdiction.** It is not in the statute (*SBI v. Videocon Industries*, NCLT Mumbai, 2019). The party seeking it must show a single economic unit. Group membership alone is not enough. Section 59A (group insolvency) has been enacted but is not yet notified.
- **Consolidation should not change the status of PMLA-attached assets** *(Compendium view, not a holding; no decided case).* The NCLT has no jurisdiction over a PMLA attachment (*Value Wise Consultancy*, NCLAT, 30.06.2026, paras 14 and 16) *(corpus: EDPORTAL_18_2026-06-30)*. A consolidated estate therefore takes the assets subject to the attachment.
- **Scrutinise consolidation applications that would help a promoter-guarantor.** The Videocon application was moved at the instance of the Chairman, whose guarantee exposure it would reduce.
- **Record, asset by asset, which group entity holds each attached asset,** so that later consolidation cannot blur the attachment.

## Part A. Personal guarantors (Chapter 17)

### A1. Is Part III valid for personal guarantors, and does an approved plan discharge the guarantee?

- ***Lalit Kumar Jain v. Union of India*** — SC, 21.05.2021, (2021) 9 SCC 321. Not in corpus. Status: binding. **Pro-ED.**
  - The notification of 15.11.2019, which brought Part III into force only for personal guarantors to corporate debtors, is a valid use of the s.1(3) power.
  - Approval of the debtor's resolution plan does not by itself discharge the guarantor. His liability arises under an independent contract of guarantee.
  - Release of the principal debtor by operation of law, or through an involuntary process, does not release the surety.
  - The compendium notes the likely counter: a particular plan's express terms may release a guarantor.
- ***SBI v. V. Ramakrishnan*** on s.31 — (2018) 17 SCC 394 : [2018] 10 SCR 974, para 25, *(as quoted in Arun Kumar Jagatramka, SC, 2021)* *(corpus: 2021_INSC_187)*. An approved plan binds the guarantor as well as the debtor. Far from helping the guarantor, s.31 is one more reason why a personal guarantor must pay.
- **How ED should use these.** The company's clean slate never reaches the promoter-guarantor personally. That is true for the bank's claim, and it is true for ED's attachment and prosecution, which never depended on the guarantee in the first place.

### A2. Does the corporate debtor's s.14 moratorium extend to the personal guarantor?

- ***State Bank of India v. V. Ramakrishnan*** (Veesons Energy Systems) — SC, 14.08.2018, (2018) 17 SCC 394 : [2018] 10 SCR 974. Not in corpus as a separate entry. Status: binding. **Pro-ED.**
  - Section 14 covers only the corporate debtor and its property.
  - Under s.14(3)(b), a surety under a guarantee of the debtor's debt cannot use the moratorium. The creditor may enforce the guarantee even while it cannot enforce the principal debt. *P. Mohanraj* para 13 cites para 20 of *V. Ramakrishnan* for this *(corpus: 2021_INSC_133)*.
  - *P. Mohanraj* distinguished *V. Ramakrishnan* on the width of "proceedings" in s.14(1)(a). It contrasted s.14 with ss.96 and 101, which govern individuals' moratoria.
- ***P. Mohanraj v. Shah Brothers Ispat*** — SC (R.F. Nariman J.), 01.03.2021, (2021) 6 SCC 258; [2021] 14 SCR 204 *(corpus: 2021_INSC_133)*. Status: binding. The NCLAT in *Value Wise* (para 13) records that its s.138 holding was referred to a larger bench in *Dineshchand Surana v. UCO Bank*. **Mixed.**
  - The moratorium applies "only to the corporate debtor". The natural persons in s.141 NI Act remain liable (para 77).
  - Confiscation of proceeds of crime is by the government "acting statutorily and not as a creditor" (para 76).
  - A moratorium only casts a shadow on proceedings. Section 32A is what extinguishes the debtor's criminal liability (paras 33-34).
- ***Alpha & Omega Diagnostics (India) Ltd. v. Asset Reconstruction Co.*** — NCLAT, 31.07.2017, CA(AT)(Ins) 116/2017. Not in corpus. Status: NCLAT. **Pro-ED (by analogy, per compendium).** Promoters' personal properties offered as security are not covered by the debtor's moratorium.
- **How ED should use these.** When a promoter-guarantor relies on the company's moratorium to resist attachment of his own assets, answer with *V. Ramakrishnan* and *P. Mohanraj* paras 76-77.

### A3. Promoters, directors and officers remain exposed under PMLA after the debtor is resolved

There is no separate case in the compendium for this point. These authorities establish it:
- ***Manish Kumar v. Union of India*** — SC, 19.01.2021, (2021) 5 SCC 1; [2021] 14 SCR 895 *(corpus: 2021_INSC_28)*. Status: binding. **Mixed.**
  - Section 32A immunity needs an approved plan and a genuine change of control to a person who is not a promoter, related party or reported accomplice (para 252).
  - Officers in default and persons involved stay liable; criminal liability "will continue to haunt" them (para 253).
  - Section 32A(2) protects only property covered by the plan. Action against the property of any other person remains open (para 254).
  - "They remain liable" (para 257). "The new management cannot be the disguised avatar of the old management" (para 258).
- ***Nitin Jain, Liquidator of PSL Ltd. v. ED*** — Delhi HC, 15.12.2021, 2021 SCC OnLine Del 5281 *(corpus: DLHC010126002021)*. Status: stands. ED's LPA was disposed of on consent on 27.02.2025, with no opinion on the law *(corpus: DLHC010404622021)*. **Adverse on the liquidation estate; helpful here.**
  - The s.32A bar in liquidation does not extend to the persons in charge (paras 98 and 101(Q)).
  - The liquidator must give PMLA authorities records and information (para 100).
- ***Bhushan Power & Steel Ltd. v. Union of India*** — Delhi HC, 30.01.2025, W.P.(Crl) 1261/2024 *(corpus: DLHC010218602024)*. Status: the order was made subject to the outcome of the appeals against the plan, which the Supreme Court decided on 26.09.2025 in *Kalyani Transco*. **Mixed.**
  - The company was discharged from the PMLA complaint under s.32A (para 7.1).
  - The Court clarified that the company's role under its old management must still be examined at the trial of the former promoters and directors (para 6.3).
- ***Arena Superstructures v. State of U.P.*** — Allahabad HC DB, 24.02.2025 *(corpus: UPHC011069462024)*. Section 32A protects only the company under new management. Letting promoters go free after plan approval would defeat the provision (paras 146-150).
- ***Ajay Kumar Radheyshyam Goenka v. Tourism Finance Corporation of India*** — SC (three judges), 15.03.2023 (per compendium). Not in corpus. Status: binding. **Pro-ED.** Section 14 does not hold s.138 prosecutions of natural persons in abeyance. Their prosecution does not end with the company's dissolution or a plan. Clauses in a plan cannot extinguish their personal liability.
- **Note.** *Rajiv Chakraborty* (Del HC, 2022) *(corpus: DLHC010046592020)* is sometimes cited for promoters' personal liability. It contains no ruling on that point. Use *Manish Kumar* and *Nitin Jain* instead.

**Procedural histories (Appendix A, per compendium; not verified unless marked):**
- ***DHFL / Piramal.***
  - RBI superseded DHFL's board in November 2019 and referred it under s.227.
  - Piramal's plan was approved on 07.06.2021, followed by a reverse merger on 03.11.2021.
  - The Bombay HC discharged the company from the CBI predicate case under s.32A on 16.11.2021.
  - The Supreme Court confirmed the plan on 01.04.2025.
  - On 02.02.2026 a PMLA Special Court granted the company s.32A relief in the connected money-laundering case [?]. It expressly preserved the prosecution of former officers and directors.
- ***Sterling Biotech / Sandesara.***
  - The CIRP was withdrawn on a promoter settlement (NCLAT, 28.08.2019), and ED and CBI proceedings were expressly preserved.
  - The promoters were declared Fugitive Economic Offenders on 28.09.2020.
  - All proceedings were later quashed by the Supreme Court on 19.11.2025, conditional on a Rs 5,100 crore deposit, in an order the Court declared non-precedential.
  - This is the fullest example of personal exposure surviving the company's settlement. The final outcome came only by an exceptional consent route.
- ***BPSL / JSW / Kalyani Transco.***
  - ED attached BPSL assets after the plan was approved, in October 2019.
  - On 11.12.2024 the Supreme Court directed a handover to JSW as restitution under s.8(8), without deciding s.32A.
  - The final judgment of 26.09.2025 (2025 INSC 1165) upheld JSW's plan.
  - The Delhi HC order above shows the company's discharge running alongside the continuing prosecution of individuals.

### A4. Consolidation applications driven by a guarantor's own interest

- **Compendium view (not a holding).** The Videocon consolidation was sought at the instance of the Chairman. Consolidation would shrink his exposure as personal guarantor. Applications should therefore be examined for whether they serve the creditors or a guarantor's personal position. See B1.

### A5. The guarantor's own Part III insolvency: ss.96 and 101

- **The law.** No decision in this set holds that the interim moratorium under s.96, or the moratorium under s.101, bars PMLA attachment or prosecution against the guarantor.
- **LawBase analysis.**
  - Sections 96 and 101 bar legal action "in respect of any debt".
  - *Axis Bank* para 141 and *P. Mohanraj* para 76 treat PMLA attachment as a sovereign penal step, not recovery of a debt. *Rajiv Chakraborty* (paras 67-69 and 78-83) applies that to s.14.
  - The same reasoning should keep PMLA action outside the Part III moratoria.
- **Risk.** *P. Mohanraj* read "proceedings" widely, and the compendium flags the s.96 argument as the guarantor's likely line.
- **How ED should use this.** Attach a promoter-guarantor's proceeds of crime before he files under Part III if possible. If he has already filed, take the point squarely and do not concede that the moratorium applies.

## Part B. Attachment across group entities and substantive consolidation (Chapter 18)

### B1. The test for substantive consolidation, and who carries the burden

- ***State Bank of India v. Videocon Industries Ltd.*** — NCLT Mumbai, 08.08.2019, M.A. 1306/2018 in C.P.(IB) 02/2018 and connected. Not in corpus. Status: NCLT, persuasive. The compendium calls it the leading Indian authority on substantive consolidation. **Neutral.**
  - The IBC has no group-insolvency provision. Even so, the NCLT may draw on its equitable jurisdiction and on comparative practice to consolidate.
  - The test has two parts. Did the companies function as a single economic unit, so intertwined that separating them is impracticable? Could they realistically attract resolution applicants separately?
  - The burden is on the party seeking consolidation. Group membership alone is not enough.
  - Thirteen of fifteen companies were consolidated. Two independent companies were kept out.
  - It does not deal with ED at all. The 2019 Working Group on Group Insolvency had suggested deferring substantive consolidation.
- **How ED should use it.** Hold the applicant to the burden. Ask whether consolidation would mix tainted and untainted assets, or reduce a promoter-guarantor's exposure.

### B2. Consolidated CIRP in practice: group insolvency on a reference under s.227

- ***Srei Infrastructure Finance Ltd. and Srei Equipment Finance Ltd., In re*** — NCLT Kolkata, 11.08.2023, I.A.(IB) 428 and 434/KB/2023 in C.P.(IB) 294 and 295/KB/2021. Not in corpus. Status: NCLT. **Pro-ED.**
  - RBI superseded the boards on 04.10.2021 and referred both NBFCs under s.227.
  - They were resolved through one CoC and one plan, which is procedural consolidation.
  - The promoters' s.12A offer of about Rs 32,000 crore was rejected.
  - NARCL's plan of Rs 14,867.50 crore was approved by 89.25% of the CoC.
  - The s.66 applications, covering about Rs 13,110 crore of flagged transactions, were left for separate decision.
- **How ED should use it.**
  - Procedural consolidation, meaning joint administration, keeps each entity's assets and liabilities separate. That is easier for ED than substantive pooling.
  - Section 66 findings in group insolvencies are tracing leads. Ask the administrator for the transaction audit.

### B3. ED has attached assets across several group entities, and those entities are then consolidated

- **The law.** There is no decided authority, and the compendium says so.
- **Compendium view (not a holding).** Consolidation cannot by itself change the status of PMLA-attached assets. The consolidated estate takes them subject to the attachment, and any challenge lies before the PMLA fora.
- **Building blocks, as checked in the corpus where possible:**
  - ***Value Wise Consultancy v. DD, ED*** — NCLAT PB, 30.06.2026 *(corpus: EDPORTAL_18_2026-06-30)*. **Pro-ED.**
    - IBC tribunals are not the forum for any plea against ED (para 14).
    - Whether to attach is for ED, and any challenge lies only in the PMLA mechanism (para 16).
    - Sections 14 and 33(5) do not reach penal proceedings over proceeds of crime (para 12).
    - The compendium cross-refers to this case as "Value Wise Consultancy / [?] Logistics". The appellant was the liquidator of Siddhi Vinayak Logistics Ltd.
  - ***Embassy Property Developments v. State of Karnataka*** — SC, 03.12.2019 [?], (2020) 13 SCC 308. Not in corpus; *Value Wise* para 14 cites it. The NCLT's jurisdiction under s.60(5) extends only so far as the working of the IBC requires. It does not include judicial review of public-law decisions.
  - ***Anil Kohli, RP of Dunar Foods v. ED*** — NCLAT, 03.07.2025 [?]. Not in corpus. Whether an asset falls in the estate depends on when the investigation began, and on the asset's link to it.
  - ***Kalyani Transco v. Bhushan Power and Steel*** — SC, 26.09.2025, 2025 INSC 1165. Not in corpus. The compendium cites it for the NCLT's lack of jurisdiction over PMLA attachments. Its own note admits the judgment does not decide that point. Use *Embassy Property* and *Value Wise* instead.
- **LawBase analysis.**
  - An order of substantive consolidation is an IBC remedy. It works on the debtors' assets and liabilities. It cannot do indirectly what the NCLT cannot do directly: release or re-attribute property that the PMLA fora hold to be proceeds of crime.
  - Pooling does not turn a tainted asset of company A into a clean asset of the consolidated estate.
  - Equivalent-value attachments are more exposed. They were made against a particular entity as "value" of its proceeds. Pooling may let lenders argue under *Axis Bank* (paras 149-165) and *Rajiv Chakraborty* (paras 110-113) that their prior security over that entity's untainted assets has priority. *Seema Garg* (P&H) *(corpus: PHHC011207472019)* may also be used to say that pre-offence assets of another group company cannot be attached as "value" at all.
  - Record clearly which entity, and which limb of s.2(1)(u), each attachment relies on.

### B4. Group-wide investigation, and consolidation sought for a collateral advantage

- **Compendium view (not a holding).** A consolidation application may deserve the same substance-over-form scrutiny that courts apply to related-party changes of control.
- **Tools:**
  - *ArcelorMittal* (SC, 2018) *(corpus: 2018_INSC_935)*: the see-through, de facto control test (paras 29 and 44-50), and the look-back to facts reasonably close in time (paras 56-57).
  - *Phoenix ARC* (SC, 2021): substance over form for related-party debt. Related status is judged at the time of the transaction.
- **How ED should use these.** Where a consolidation would put a connected party on the CoC, would benefit a promoter-guarantor, or would mask intra-group diversion, place the investigation material before the NCLT. The NCLT cannot rule on the attachment. It can, however, refuse the equitable remedy if the material shows it is being sought for a collateral purpose.

## Open points and risks

1. **Part III moratoria (ss.96 and 101).** Whether they reach PMLA attachment of a guarantor's assets is undecided. Expect this to be argued. The *Axis Bank* and *P. Mohanraj* para 76 reasoning is ED's answer.
2. **Express plan terms releasing a guarantor.** *Lalit Kumar Jain* leaves room for a plan to provide otherwise. Even so, a plan cannot bind the State's penal powers over a guarantor's proceeds of crime, because ED is not a creditor bound under s.31 *(LawBase analysis)*. See also the open question on s.31(6) below.
3. **Consolidation and attachment.** There is no authority. The compendium's reading is not settled law. Lenders may use consolidation to reopen priority over equivalent-value attachments.
4. ***P. Mohanraj* referral.** If the larger bench in *Dineshchand Surana* narrows *P. Mohanraj*'s reading of "proceedings", that may affect the Part III argument. Para 77, on natural persons, and para 76, on ED not being a creditor, are separate points.
5. ***Videocon* is a single NCLT decision.** It rests on equitable jurisdiction.
6. **The DHFL Special Court order of 02.02.2026** is known only from the compendium, and the operative word is garbled [?]. Read the order before relying on it.

## After the 2026 Amendment

The IBC (Amendment) Act, 2026 (Act 6 of 2026) brought most provisions into force on 26.05.2026 (S.O. 2625(E) of 22.05.2026).
- **Section 28A (new): transfer of guarantor assets.** It is in force. We have not read its text in LawBase. Before relying on any argument about guarantor assets dealt with in a plan, check whether s.28A lets a plan or the CoC deal with a guarantor's assets. If it does, check whether it says anything about property under attachment. *LawBase analysis:* any such transfer should be subject to an existing PMLA attachment. The NCLT has no jurisdiction over that attachment (*Value Wise* paras 14 and 16), and s.32A(2), which is unamended, protects only the corporate debtor's property covered by the plan (*Manish Kumar* para 254).
- **Section 14(3)(b) now carries an Explanation on sureties.** Read it with *V. Ramakrishnan*. Nothing in it is reported to bring PMLA action within the moratorium.
- **Section 59A (group insolvency) is enacted but not notified.** Until it is notified, group consolidation continues to rest on *Videocon*-type equitable jurisdiction and on procedural coordination, as in *Srei*. When it is notified, check whether it says anything about property held under other laws. Where it is silent, the *Value Wise* reasoning should continue to apply.
- **Chapter IV-A (creditor-initiated insolvency, ss.58A-58K) and ss.240B-240C (cross-border) are also enacted but not notified.**
- **Section 31(6)** extinguishes pre-approval claims "against the corporate debtor and its assets". On its terms it does not reach a guarantor's personal assets. Whether it reaches a PMLA attachment of the debtor's assets is an open question. Section 32A is unchanged.
