**P. Mohanraj & Ors. v. M/s. Shah Brothers Ispat Pvt. Ltd.**, [2021] 14 SCR 204 : 2021 INSC 133, Civil Appeal No. 10355 of 2018 (R.F. Nariman, Navin Sinha and K.M. Joseph, JJ.; judgment by R.F. Nariman, J.; 01.03.2021). Paragraph numbers below follow the SCR text in the corpus. SCC OnLine numbering differs: SCR para 76 is cited as para 100 in later High Court judgments.

**Held.**
- **Para 28.** Section 14(1)(a) "proceedings" is given a wide meaning that fits its object. Counsel conceded, and the Court recorded, that "criminal proceedings which are not directly related to transactions evidencing debt or liability of the corporate debtor would be outside the scope of this expression".
- **Paras 33-34 (s.32A distinguished).** Section 32A extinguishes the corporate debtor's criminal liability once a plan is approved. A moratorium "does not extinguish any liability, civil or criminal, but only casts a shadow on proceedings", and that shadow lifts when the moratorium ends. "Prosecution" in the first proviso to s.32A(1) means "criminal proceedings properly so-called", started by an FIR or by a complaint of an investigating authority. It does not cover a quasi-criminal s.138 complaint.
- **Para 43.** A s.138 proceeding is a "civil sheep" in a "criminal wolf's" clothing. It protects the payee's interest, not the larger interest of the State.
- **Para 76 (PMLA).** The Court considered the Delhi High Court's ruling in *ED v. Axis Bank* (2019) and agreed with its reasoning that the State does not act as a creditor when it attaches proceeds of crime. "The confiscation of the proceeds of crime is by the government acting statutorily and not as a creditor."
- **Para 77.** The moratorium applies "only to the corporate debtor". The persons named in s.141(1) and (2) NI Act remain liable and can be proceeded against during the moratorium.
- **Para 78.** A s.138/141 proceeding against a corporate debtor is covered by s.14(1)(a). Contrary Bombay and Calcutta High Court rulings were not followed.
- **Para 79.** On the facts, the proceedings were allowed to continue against both the company and the directors. The moratorium had ended, and there was no change of management.

**IBC-PMLA use.**
- RPs often cite this case to argue that the moratorium suspends PMLA action. The answer comes from the judgment itself:
  - its reasoning rests on the compensatory, debt-recovery character of s.138 (paras 28 and 43);
  - para 76 accepts that PMLA confiscation is a sovereign statutory act, not a creditor's claim; and
  - para 33 shows that only s.32A, not s.14, can end an offence-related liability.
- Use para 77 against any director, promoter or KMP who claims the corporate debtor's moratorium as a personal shield.
- *Rajiv Chakraborty, RP of EIEL v. ED* (Delhi HC, 11.11.2022; corpus DLHC010046592020), paras 79-80, reads this judgment as covering only proceedings to enforce or recover a debt of the corporate debtor. On that basis it holds that PMLA attachment is outside s.14. Cite the two judgments together.

**After the 2026 Amendment.** Section 14 is now subject to the new s.14(2A) on critical supplies and to an Explanation in s.14(3)(b) on sureties. Neither change brings PMLA attachment or prosecution within the moratorium. Paras 33 and 76 are unaffected.

**Later history (per compendium).**
- Distinguished for PMLA purposes by *Rajiv Chakraborty* (Delhi HC).
- Applied to natural persons in *Ajay Kumar Radheyshyam Goenka v. Tourism Finance Corporation of India* (SC, 15.03.2023; not in the corpus). That decision holds that prosecution of signatories and directors survives dissolution of the company and approval of a plan.
- The compendium says this judgment left the position of natural persons open. The SCR text does not support that reading, because para 77 decides the point.
